Wave Race 64 Water Study                                   Project Plan


                          WATER GRID STUDY


Abstract

   A standalone Direct3D 9 program that reproduces one effect from Wave
   Race 64 and nothing else: the hexagonal water grid as the race
   results screen shows it, viewed head on. It runs the original
   simulation on the original mesh with the original textures, but
   reaches the screen through D3D9 vertices rather than an N64 display
   list, and it is driven by a real clock so the motion is correct at 60
   Hz and above.

   The point of the exercise is the time base. The N64 scheme is a
   fixed-step integer integrator; getting it to behave at an arbitrary
   display rate is the part that needs design, and section 6 is the
   substance of this plan.

1.  Goal And Scope

   Open a window. Draw the Wave Race 64 water grid in the middle of it.
   Let the waves propagate. Orbit and zoom the view. Click the water and
   make a wave.

   Three things make this worth doing separately from the port:

   o  NO DISPLAY LISTS. The port builds N64 command lists and runs them
      through a software RSP. Here the mesh data goes straight into a
      D3D9 vertex buffer, so the simulation can be read and reasoned
      about without a graphics pipeline in the way.

   o  A REAL CLOCK. The port inherits the N64's one-tick-per-frame
      assumption. This program separates simulation rate from display
      rate deliberately, which is the only way the motion is right on a
      144 Hz panel.

   o  ONE EFFECT. No course, no boat, no HUD, no audio. When the water
      looks wrong there is nothing else it could be.


2.  What Is Being Reproduced

   The results screen sets sea_mode = 1, and that selects the largest of
   the three water configurations (ot_sea.c:1823):

   +---------------------+-------+------------------------------------+
   | constant            | value | role                               |
   +=====================+=======+====================================+
   | sea_disp_blocks (N) |    16 | hex radius in cells                |
   | sea_disp_points     |   817 | vertices, 3*N*(N+1)+1              |
   | sea_disp_size       |  1024 | world units across, N << 6         |
   | suii_power          |    10 | shift for the averaging term       |
   | wave_power          |     4 | shift for the difference term      |
   | water_damp          |     5 | shift for the velocity decay       |
   +---------------------+-------+------------------------------------+

   N = 16 is the tight case: position_t is dimensioned 817, which is
   exactly 3*N*(N+1)+1 at N = 16, so the mesh has no spare entry.

   The in-race modes use N = 12, or N = 10 when sea_mode is 2 or two
   players are racing, and 9/5/6 for the shifts. Read the directions
   carefully, because they do not all point the same way: a LARGER
   wave_power means a smaller difference term and so SLOWER propagation,
   and a LARGER water_damp subtracts less and so lets waves PERSIST
   LONGER. So in-race water propagates more slowly and lasts longer than
   the results screen's, while the results screen has the weaker
   restoring term of the two. Confirmed live on the ranking screen:
   sea_mode 0, N 12, sea_disp_points 469, shifts 9/5/6.

   The results screen is the one that shows the grid head on with the
   patch centred at the origin and no camera motion, which is why it is
   the target.

   THE TICK RATE IS 30 Hz ON THIS SCREEN, NOT 20. The graph thread is
   woken once every Graph_timing vertical retraces, so the simulation
   rate is 60/Graph_timing (ot_pctask.c:294):

       if (fc - frame_count_bak >= Graph_timing) {
           frame_count_bak = fc;
           Graph_timing    = Graph_timing_req;
           osSendMesg(&MesgQ_T2G_timing, ...);
       }

   kn_main.h sets the divisor per screen, and the two that matter differ:

   +------------------------+-----+--------+--------------------------+
   | constant               | div | rate   | used by                  |
   +========================+=====+========+==========================+
   | GRAPH_TIMING_RACE      |   3 |  20 Hz | the race, machine select |
   | GRAPH_TIMING_RESULT    |   2 |  30 Hz | results, course, record  |
   +------------------------+-----+--------+--------------------------+

   So the race physics runs at 20 Hz, and that is the figure to quote
   for the game as a whole, but initMainGlistResult and
   kn_psresult.c:728 both ask for GRAPH_TIMING_RESULT, which is 2. The
   water on the results screen therefore steps at 30 Hz.

   That pairing is consistent: sea_mode 1 uses the gentler shifts
   (suii 10, wave 4, damp 5) at 30 Hz, while the race uses suii 9,
   wave 5, damp 6 at 20 Hz with a smaller grid. The constants were tuned
   against the rate the screen runs at, which is why SIM_DT has to be
   the screen's rate and not the game's headline rate.

       SIM_DT = 1.0 / 30.0        /* 33.333 ms */

   See _wr64/docs/subsystems/ot_sea_report.txt for the full subsystem
   write-up. This plan restates only what the program needs.


3.  Source Material

3.1.  The Shell, From MOTORACER

   n:/DEV/MOTORACER has a working D3D9 host that already does everything
   this program needs before it draws any water. Take it rather than
   write another one.

    +----------------+-------------------------------------------------+
    | file           | what it provides                                |
    +================+=================================================+
    | src/main.cpp   | AllocConsole, window class, WinMain, the        |
    |                | frame loop, and the pacer                       |
    | src/d3d9rt.cpp | dynamic Direct3DCreate9, device create          |
    |                | and reset, depth format choice,                 |
    |                | letterboxing                                    |
    | src/timing.h   | tm_init, tm_now, tm_real_now                    |
    | src/timing.c   | QueryPerformanceCounter, pause and frame        |
    |                | step                                            |
    +----------------+-------------------------------------------------+

   Two details of that code matter here:

   o  R_FVF3 is already the untransformed format this program wants:
      D3DFVF_XYZ | D3DFVF_DIFFUSE | D3DFVF_SPECULAR | D3DFVF_TEX1, with
      R_Vertex3 as {float x,y,z; uint color; uint specular; float
      tu,tv;}. Geometry is submitted in camera space and D3D9 clips it
      against a real frustum.

   o  Stage 0 is a fixed-function combiner stage, which is all the sea
      pass needs: its N64 combiner is TEXEL0 alone. No shader work.
      See section 9.1 - the stage was set to MODULATE(TEXTURE, DIFFUSE)
      until the combiner words were decoded, and that was wrong.

   MOTORACER's own docs/subsystems/frame-loop.txt is worth reading
   before section 6: it records that its race takes a whole frame delta
   as one physics step, and what that cost them.

   list construction with it.

   | make_data_table          | builds index_t and position_t, the   |
   |                          | hex layout; run once                 |
   | init_wave_high           | seeds the surface with random cell   |
   |                          | heights                              |

   Deliberately left out: plus_sea and friends (buoyancy, no boat here),

3.3.  Textures

   Eight sea textures, one per weather theme, in
   _wr64/src_n64/assets/tex_data/_recovered_data/:

     sot_sea_dirt    sot_sea_eve     sot_sea_fine1   sot_sea_fine2
     sot_sea_morn    sot_sea_night   sot_sea_sto     sot_sea_ice

   THAT ORDER IS NOT ALPHABETICAL AND MUST NOT BE SORTED. It is
   sea_tex_list's, because crs_sea[] indexes into it by position; see
   section 9.3.

   Each is an unsigned int name[1024], and the DL loads them with
   G_SETTIMG 0xFD180000, which decodes to G_IM_FMT_RGBA and
   G_IM_SIZ_32b. So each is 32 x 32 RGBA8888, 4096 bytes. The first word
   of sot_sea_fine1 is 0xBEDADB7D: R 190, G 218, B 219, A 125.

   Conversion is a swizzle. N64 packs RGBA in that byte order; D3D9
   A8R8G8B8 wants ARGB.

   All eight are EMBEDDED, in src/textures.c, derived from the arrays
   above and verified byte-identical to them. They are unsigned char in
   the N64's own R G B A order, so the data carries no endianness and
   r_texture_upload does the swizzle per channel at load time. The
   program reads no files at all; T steps through the courses.

   The converter that produced them has been removed. src/textures.c is
   now the only copy, so treat it as data: regenerating it means writing
   the unpacker again against the arrays above.

   There are also sot_sea_*_2p variants under assets/texture/ for the
   split-screen case. Not needed.


4.  Data Model

4.1.  The Simulation Buffer

   BigWaveCell is four bytes:

     typedef struct { short height; short flags; } BigWaveCell;

   height is the surface displacement in fixed point. flags is the
   propagation accumulator, which behaves as a velocity. The buffer is
   wave[384][128], 196608 bytes, and cells are addressed by a byte
   offset computed as

     offset = (((row % 384) << 7) | (col & 0x7F)) << 2

   The masking is what makes the offset safe without a range check: the
   result cannot leave the 384 x 128 array.

   The buffer is far larger than the 817 visible cells because the
   in-race patch scrolls across it. This program does not scroll, so a
   smaller buffer would do; keep the original dimensions anyway so the
   offset arithmetic is unchanged and the two can be compared.

4.2.  The Visible Mesh

   Two parallel tables, both built once:

     typedef struct { int offset; short sx; short sy; } SeaScreenEntry;
     typedef struct { unsigned char dx, dy; } SeaDispOffset;

     SeaScreenEntry position_t[817];
     SeaDispOffset  index_t[820];

   sx and sy are the patch-local XZ of each mesh corner, fixed at init.
   offset is the byte offset of that corner's cell in wave[], refreshed
   every frame in the original because the patch moves. Here it can be
   computed once, which is a simplification worth making explicit in the
   code rather than silently.

   Layout, per row of the hexagon: sx values are spaced 64 world units;
   odd rows stagger sx by -32; row width is 2*N - abs(row). That yields
   the 817 points, and the count is the centred hexagonal number
   3*N*(N+1)+1.

4.3.  Vertex Attributes

   Per mesh corner, the original bakes:

     world X = patch_center_x + sx
     world Y = (cell.height >> 8) + sea_offset
     world Z = patch_center_z + sy

   and, for interior corners, a slope term from the finite difference of
   the neighbouring cells' heights.

   THAT SLOPE TERM IS NOT A LIGHTING NORMAL. It feeds generated texture
   coordinates, an environment-map trick, and it is what gives Wave
   Race's water its sheen. Reproducing the look means driving tu/tv from
   the slope, not lighting a normal; per-vertex lighting instead will
   look plausible and wrong. Section 9.2 has the mapping.


5.  The Algorithm

   Once, at startup:

     build index_t and position_t      (make_data_table)
     seed cell heights                 (init_wave_high)

   Per simulation tick:

     wave_sea()      propagate: three phases over the hex, walking
                     position_t in order. Per coupled pair,

                       avg  = (h_a + h_b) >> suii_power
                       diff = (h_b - h_a) >> wave_power
                       a.flags += diff - avg
                       b.flags -= diff + avg

                     then per cell, fold velocity into position and
                     decay it,

                       cell.height += cell.flags
                       cell.flags  -= cell.flags >> water_damp

     The three phases are the upper hex rows (widths N to 2N-1), the
     lower rows (2N down to N+1), and a tip strip. The cursor advances
     exactly 3*N*(N+1) positions, 816 at N = 16, one short of the 817
     entries, so the walk cannot run off the end.

   Per injection, from a click:

     push_sea(px, pz, depth)   find the three cells of the containing
                               triangle, write height, zero flags

   Per rendered frame:

     bake 817 vertices into R_Vertex3, build the index list, draw


6.  Time

6.1.  Why The Original Scheme Will Not Take A Variable Step

   The three lines above are a fixed-step explicit integrator with the
   step length baked in as 1. Each of them breaks differently if the
   step changes:

   o  THE DECAY IS PER-STEP, NOT PER-SECOND. `flags -= flags >> 5`
      multiplies the velocity by 31/32 every tick. Under a variable step
      the correct factor is pow(31/32, dt/dt0), and simply scaling the
      subtraction by dt is not the same function.

   o  THE INTEGRATION ASSUMES dt = 1. `height += flags` is explicit
      Euler with the step folded into the units of flags, so height and
      flags are not independently meaningful in seconds.

   o  THE COUPLING SETS THE WAVE SPEED, AND WITH IT A STABILITY LIMIT.
      The `>> wave_power` difference term is a discrete Laplacian;
      explicit integration of a wave equation is only stable below a
      step size that depends on it. Feeding a 6 ms step and a 40 ms step
      into the same constants changes both the wave speed and whether
      the system stays bounded.

   o  THE ARITHMETIC IS INTEGER. The shifts are exact only for the
      shifts the original chose. Rescaling them per frame reintroduces
      rounding that the fixed-point design assumed away.

   So the honest position is that the original cannot be handed a
   variable dt without being re-derived, and re-deriving it risks
   changing the character of the water, which is the one thing this
   program exists to preserve.

6.2.  The Fixed-Step Accumulator

   Run the simulation at its native rate and let the display rate float.
   This is the standard fixed-timestep pump:

     accumulator += real_dt            (from tm_real_now)
     steps = 0
     while (accumulator >= SIM_DT && steps < MAX_STEPS) {
         save previous heights
         wave_sea()
         accumulator -= SIM_DT
         steps++
     }
     if (steps == MAX_STEPS)
         accumulator = 0            /* give up the backlog */

   What this buys: the integer arithmetic is untouched, so the water is
   bit-identical to the original tick for tick; the display can present
   at any rate; and nothing about stability or wave speed needs retuning.

   MAX_STEPS exists to stop the spiral of death. If a stall leaves 400
   ms in the accumulator, catching up with 12 steps costs more than a
   frame and the backlog grows. Cap it, drop the remainder, and accept
   that a hitch slows the water briefly rather than locking the program.
   Eight is a reasonable cap.

   SIM_DT is 1/30 s on this screen; see section 2 for where that comes
   from and why it is not the game's 20 Hz race rate.

6.3.  Render Interpolation

   A fixed sim rate under a faster display rate shows as judder unless
   the renderer interpolates. Keep the previous tick's 817 heights and
   blend:

     alpha = accumulator / SIM_DT          /* 0 .. 1 */
     y     = lerp(prev_height[i], curr_height[i], alpha)

   Two height arrays of 817 shorts is 3.2 KB, so the cost is nothing.
   Interpolate only for the bake; the simulation keeps stepping on
   integers.

   The slope term should be computed from the interpolated heights, not
   carried from the last tick, or the sheen will step at the sim rate
   while the surface moves smoothly.

6.4.  Continuous-Time Reformulation

   A stretch goal, and only once 6.2 looks right, because 6.2 is the
   reference it has to be judged against.

   Restate the scheme in floats with time in seconds: velocity decay as
   an exponential, the coupling as a wave-equation term with an explicit
   speed, and a substep count chosen from the CFL limit so the step is
   always stable. Then measure, rather than eyeball: run both for a
   fixed number of seconds from the same seed and the same injections,
   and compare the 817 heights by RMS error.

   If it diverges, the fixed-step version stays and this becomes a note
   about why. That is a perfectly good outcome; the goal is correct
   motion at high refresh, and 6.2 already delivers that.


7.  Milestones

   Each one ends with something on screen, and each is a place to stop.

   M0  WINDOW. main.cpp and d3d9rt.cpp lifted from MOTORACER, console
       plus a cleared D3D9 window, timing module in, fps on the console.

   M1  MESH. make_data_table ported, 817 vertices, drawn as wireframe or
       points, flat. Verifies the hex topology and the point count
       before anything moves.

   M2  TEXTURE. One sea texture converted and applied, triangle list
       built, flat lit water. Verifies winding, UVs and the modulate
       stage.

   M3  MOTION. wave_sea ported, fixed-step accumulator per 6.2, render
       interpolation per 6.3, a key to inject a wave. The first point at
       which it should look like Wave Race.

   M4  VIEW. Orbit and zoom on the mouse, with the head-on results-screen
       framing as the default and a key to return to it.

   M5  PICKING. Done. X held unprojects the cursor onto the water plane
       and calls sea_push, stamping from the previous sample to the
       current one so a drag leaves a ridge rather than a dotted line.
       pick_water inverts exactly the projection r_set_camera builds;
       the two share kFovY so they cannot drift apart. sea_contains
       bounds it, because sea_push wraps rather than misses.

   M6  SLOPE SHEEN. Done. Section 9 records what the original does and
       what was built: the sphere-map coordinates, the translucent
       blend, and the eight per-course textures on T.

   M7  STRETCH. The continuous-time reformulation of 6.4, with the RMS
       comparison against M3 as its acceptance test. C selects 20, 30
       or 60 Hz, which makes the problem visible rather than solving
       it: the state depends on the tick count and not on elapsed
       time, so 60 Hz for one second, 30 for two and 20 for three all
       reach the same surface. Measured, not assumed.


8.  Non-Goals

   o  No N64 display lists, no software RSP, no gbi.h.
   o  No boat, buoyancy, course geometry, HUD, audio or input replay.
   o  No scrolling patch. The results screen centres it at the origin
      and leaves it there; the camera-follow and corner-ring
      interpolation in move_sea_one and vertex_sea are out of scope.
   o  No 2-player split screen, and none of the _2p texture variants.
   o  No attempt at a byte-identical match to the ROM. This is a study
      of the effect, not a decompilation; _wr64 is where fidelity work
      belongs.


9.  The Look

   The water does not look like a textured grid because it is not one.
   It is a translucent sphere-mapped surface blended over the course
   background, and all three of those words carry weight: get any one
   of them wrong and the sheen does not appear.

9.1.  What The Original Sets

   make_sea_glist, _wr64/src_n64/sot_world.c, builds the state by hand
   as raw command words. Decoded:

   +----------------------+------------+------------------------------+
   | Command              | Word       | Meaning                      |
   +======================+============+==============================+
   | G_SETGEOMETRYMODE    | 0x00000005 | G_ZBUFFER and G_SHADE only.  |
   |                      |            | No G_TEXTURE_GEN and no      |
   |                      |            | G_LIGHTING, so the RSP is    |
   |                      |            | not generating the texture   |
   |                      |            | coordinates. vertex_sea is.  |
   +----------------------+------------+------------------------------+
   | G_SETCOMBINE         | 0xFCFFFFFF | RGB = TEXEL0, A = TEXEL0_A,  |
   |                      | 0xFFFCF279 | in both cycles. The texture  |
   |                      |            | alone: no shade term and no  |
   |                      |            | environment term.            |
   +----------------------+------------+------------------------------+
   | G_SETOTHERMODE_H     | 0x00000000 | G_CYC_1CYCLE.                |
   +----------------------+------------+------------------------------+
   | G_SETOTHERMODE_L     | 0x005049D8 | G_RM_AA_ZB_XLU_SURF. Z_CMP   |
   |                      |            | set, Z_UPD CLEAR, FORCE_BL,  |
   |                      |            | ZMODE_XLU, and the blender   |
   |                      |            | CLR_IN*A_IN + CLR_MEM*(1-A). |
   +----------------------+------------+------------------------------+
   | G_SETTILE            | 0x00080200 | cm_s = cm_t = CLAMP, mask 0. |
   |                      |            | The texture does not tile.   |
   +----------------------+------------+------------------------------+
   | G_SETTILESIZE        | 0x0007C07C | 32 by 32 texels.             |
   +----------------------+------------+------------------------------+
   | G_TEXTURE            | 0x80008000 | S and T scale 0.5.           |
   +----------------------+------------+------------------------------+

   G_SETENVCOLOR is also issued, carrying crs_color[0].env replicated
   into all four bytes, but no combiner in the sea pass reads ENV. It is
   for a later list in the chain. Only course MARIN takes the 2-cycle
   branch and sets a fog colour from sea_fog_r/g/b/a; every other course
   runs the 1-cycle path above.

   The consequence that matters most: Z_UPD is clear and the blend is
   alpha over the framebuffer. The water is TRANSLUCENT. Its alpha comes
   per-texel from the texture and runs as low as 71 of 255, so a good
   part of what the eye reads as water is the background seen through
   it. An opaque draw cannot look like this however right the texture
   coordinates are.

9.2.  The Sphere Map

   vertex_sea generates tu and tv per vertex from the surface slope.
   This is the chrome trick from the SGI demos: the texture is not a
   picture of water laid on the surface, it is a picture of the
   surroundings indexed by which way the surface faces.

   Per vertex, with heights read as shorts and shifted right 7:

       hA = height of the point one row back,  32 units left
       hB = height of the point one row on,    32 units left
       hC = height of the next point in this row

       sx = (((hA + hB) >> 1) - hC) << 6
       sy = 55 * (hA - hB)
       d  = sqrt(sx*sx + sy*sy + 12582912)

       tc[0] = sx * (992 / d) + 1024
       tc[1] = sy * (992 / d) + 1024

   That is a lattice cross product. 64 is the column pitch and 55 the
   row pitch, 64*sqrt(3)/2 rounded; 12582912 is 3547 squared, and
   64 * 55.4 is 3547, so the constant is the up component in the same
   units as the other two. So (sx, sy, 3547) is the unnormalised surface
   normal, and tc is its XY after normalising, scaled by 992 and centred
   on 1024.

   Texture coordinates are S10.5, G_TEXTURE halves them, and the texture
   is 32 texels, so u = tc/2048 and the whole mapping reduces to

       u = 0.5 + 0.484 * nx
       v = 0.5 + 0.484 * ny

   over a single non-tiled image. Flat water samples the middle texel
   and the surface is one flat colour. A tilting wave face sweeps the
   sample across the whole 32 by 32 picture, and since that picture is
   bright at one end and dark at the other, the face lights up and goes
   out as it rolls. With CLAMP holding the edges and the per-texel alpha
   letting the background through, that is the glass.

   THE NEIGHBOUR PAIR IS NOT THE OBVIOUS ONE. vertex_sea has three loop
   phases and each uses a different index stride:

   +-------------------+---------------+---------------+
   | Rows              | hA at         | hB at         |
   +===================+===============+===============+
   | -(N-1) .. -1      | i - w(r)      | i + w(r)      |
   +-------------------+---------------+---------------+
   | 0, the middle row | i - w(r)      | i + w(r) - 1  |
   +-------------------+---------------+---------------+
   | 1 .. N-1          | i - w(r) - 1  | i + w(r) - 1  |
   +-------------------+---------------+---------------+

   where w(r) is row r's width. Three formulas, one geometric meaning:
   each picks the two points in the adjacent rows that sit 32 units to
   the left of the centre, and so are level with each other. The strides
   differ only because the row stagger reverses across the middle row.
   sea.c builds the pair from that geometric rule instead, having
   checked it reproduces all three formulas at every interior point.

   The first and last rows get no normal in the original, which leaves
   their coordinates at whatever the vertex buffer held. sea.c gives
   them a flat normal, so the rim samples the middle texel. That is a
   deliberate divergence, and it is visible only at the outermost ring.

9.3.  Per-Course Colour

   There is no per-course tint constant. Each course selects one of
   eight 32 by 32 RGBA8888 textures through crs_sea[] in
   _wr64/src_n64/assets/texture/live_textures.c, which copies it into
   the live buffer sea_tex0 that the display list points at.

   +---------------------+----------------+--------------------------+
   | Course              | Texture        | Mean RGB                 |
   +=====================+================+==========================+
   | DOLPHIN, SANDY      | sot_sea_fine1  | (194, 218, 219)          |
   +---------------------+----------------+--------------------------+
   | SUNSET              | sot_sea_eve    | (235, 160, 108)  orange  |
   +---------------------+----------------+--------------------------+
   | MARIN               | sot_sea_sto    | (182, 202, 217)          |
   +---------------------+----------------+--------------------------+
   | MILKY               | sot_sea_morn   | (183, 222, 220)          |
   +---------------------+----------------+--------------------------+
   | PORT                | sot_sea_dirt   | (133, 158, 170)          |
   +---------------------+----------------+--------------------------+
   | CASTLE              | sot_sea_night  | ( 34,  50,  59)  dark    |
   +---------------------+----------------+--------------------------+
   | SOUTHERN            | sot_sea_fine2  | (173, 223, 241)  cyan    |
   +---------------------+----------------+--------------------------+

   +---------------------+----------------+--------------------------+

   So the sunset-orange water, the tropical cyan and the near-black are
   three of these eight images, not three settings. T cycles them.

   Alpha varies per texel in seven of the eight, from as low as 71 up
   to 255. sot_sea_dirt is the exception, a flat 176, which is why PORT
   reads as uniformly murky rather than glassy.

9.4.  The Backdrop Is A Flat Per-Course Fill

   The water is translucent, so most of what the eye reads as its colour
   is whatever is behind it - and behind it is not a texture. It is one
   flat rectangle. makeKnSeaBottomGlist, kn_main.c:

       sb_p = &mSeaBottomColorTable[course_num];
       gSPDisplayList( gp++, dlDispSeaBottom );
       gDPSetPrimColor( gp++, 0, 0, sb_p->r, sb_p->g, sb_p->b, 255 );
       gDPFillRectangle( gp++, DISPLAY_X1, DISPLAY_Y1,
                               DISPLAY_X2, DISPLAY_Y2 );

   It fills the whole display rect in the course's sea-bed colour, and
   the world and then the water are drawn over it. Running the port
   windowed shows this plainly: the 4:3 area carries the colour and the
   letterbox borders do not.

   +------------+----------------+-----------------+
   | Course     | sea texture    | sea bed colour  |
   +============+================+=================+
   | DOLPHIN    | sot_sea_fine1  | 0, 242, 228     |
   +------------+----------------+-----------------+
   | SANDY      | sot_sea_fine1  | 0, 242, 228     |

   +------------+----------------+-----------------+

   | PORT       | sot_sea_dirt   | 80, 117, 85     |
   +------------+----------------+-----------------+

   Texture from crs_sea[] in live_textures.c, colour from

   THERE IS NO ENVIRONMENT LIGHT. The sea combiner is TEXEL0 alone, as

10.  Drawing Waves

   X held unprojects the cursor onto the water plane and calls sea_push
   along the path, so a drag leaves a ridge. Three numbers control the
   stamp, and the wheel tweaks them while A, S or D is held - shift
   makes a notch worth ten. R puts all three back.

   +-----------+-----+-----+--------+----------------------------------+
   | Value     | Key | Def | Range  | What it is                       |
   +===========+=====+=====+========+==================================+
   | depth     | D   | 64  | 1..120 | Height, before the << 8 into     |
   |           |     |     |        | fixed point. The ceiling is not  |
   |           |     |     |        | a matter of taste; see 10.1.     |
   +-----------+-----+-----+--------+----------------------------------+
   | spacing   | -   | 32  | fixed  | World units between stamps along |
   |           |     |     |        | a drag.                          |
   +-----------+-----+-----+--------+----------------------------------+
   | step cap  | -   | 64  | fixed  | Most stamps one mouse message    |
   |           |     |     |        | may place.                       |
   +-----------+-----+-----+--------+----------------------------------+

   ONLY DEPTH IS A KNOB, and the other two were measured before being
   demoted rather than guessed at.

   The loop is for (k = 1; k < steps; k++) with steps = len / spacing,
   so it places steps - 1 points and a steps of 0 or 1 places none. At
   the home view one pixel is 2.5 world units, which makes the threshold

       spacing  32 -> 25.6 px of travel in one mouse message
       spacing  16 -> 12.8 px
       spacing   4 ->  3.2 px

   and Windows delivers mouse messages at about 125 Hz, so an ordinary
   drag advances a few pixels per message and the interpolation never
   fires. It does not need to: one sea_push disturbs 64 to 128 world
   units and consecutive samples land 5 to 25 apart, so the stamps
   already overlap almost completely. Only a fast flick - 200 px in one
   message is 500 world units, eight cells - leaves a gap worth filling.

   The step cap is further still from mattering. It clamps only when
   len / spacing exceeds 64, which at spacing 32 means a single message
   carrying 2048 world units: the whole width of the mesh. It is a
   runaway guard for a cursor teleport, not a tuning value.

10.1.  Why Depth Stops At 120

   sea_push stores the height as

       (short)((unsigned short)fdp << 8)

   so only the low 8 bits of the depth survive the shift. 127 is the
   last value that stores a positive height; 128 to 255 read back
   NEGATIVE, and any multiple of 256 stores exactly zero. A depth of
   6400 is 0x1900, which shifts to 0x190000, whose low 16 bits are 0 -
   it does nothing at all, silently.

   The relaxation lowers the ceiling again. A stamp of 127 is 32512 of
   a signed short's 32767, and wave_sea then adds to it: a dense drag
   at 127 drives eight cells over the rail and wraps them negative,
   which reads as sudden pits. Measured peak height against depth, over
   200 ticks of a hexagon-filling stamp:

       depth  64 -> peak 17273    no wraps
       depth 100 -> peak 26898    no wraps
       depth 110 -> peak 29597    no wraps
       depth 120 -> peak 32278    no wraps
       depth 127 -> peak 32721    8 wraps

   So 120, not 127.

10.2.  The push_sea Row Term

   DIVERGENCE FROM THE ORIGINAL, deliberate, and the reason is not what
   it first looks like.

   push_sea adds (v4 & 0xFFFFFF80) to the row index, where v4 is the hex
   column. The disassembly confirms it: push_sea at ot_sea.o 0x2DCC has

       li   at,-0x80
       and  t6,a1,at        a1 is the column
       addu t2,a0,t6        a0 is the row

   a real mask, not an sra by 7. So the decompile is faithful.

   THE ROM IS NOT BUGGY. ot_sea.c:2481, which builds the mesh corner
   offsets, adds the same term: (v5 & 0xFFFFFF80) where v5 is that
   corner's column. Both ends carry the column's high bits into the row,
   so both agree. Checked over five patch positions including negative
   ones: 4085 of 4085 corners reachable. It is an odd scheme - a carry
   out of a 7-bit column field moves 128 rows, not one - but it is
   consistent, and consistency is all it needs.

   What diverged was THIS PORT. sea_build is the algorithm of
   make_data_table rather than a transcription of it, and it dropped the
   carry: offset was simply row (r+1536)%384, column dx&127. Against a
   faithful push_sea that left only 457 of 817 corners reachable, with
   the dead region bounded by the line dx = 0 through the centre at 30
   degrees to the z axis - a wedge, in a staggered lattice, rather than
   a half. That was the bug, and it was ours.

   BOTH ENDS NOW CARRY IT, as the original does. sea_build takes
   ot_sea.c:2481 with the patch at the origin, which makes its col_base
   and row_mod zero and leaves (dx & ~0x7F) + r + 1536. The mesh reads
   817 distinct cells across rows 0..16, 240..271 and 368..383 - that
   middle block is the dx < 0 half, 128 rows away, exactly where the
   original puts it - and sea_push reaches all 817.

   Either end alone is wrong; the pair has to match. Restoring rather
   than simplifying costs one term and buys the scrolling patch, which
   is the only thing the extra rows are for.

10.3.  The Walker On The Menu Screens

   On every sea_mode 1 screen the water has something crossing it, and
   it is not a rider with the model switched off. move_sea runs a single
   invisible point across the patch, and that point is the ONLY thing
   disturbing the water there:

       step    51.2 world units per tick, 0.8 of the 64-unit cell
               pitch, so successive dimples overlap into a wake rather
               than a dotted line
       heading r, a 12-bit angle into sin_t[4096]; the +1024 index
               offset is +90 degrees, so one table serves as sine and
               cosine both
       turn    r += ra every tick, so the path is always an arc. ra
               lands in -7..+6 twelve-bit units, which is -0.615 to
               +0.527 degrees per tick - a full circle would take about
               19 seconds, so over a crossing it reads as very nearly
               straight
       depth   push_sea(px, pz, -50), NEGATIVE. It digs a trough, not a
               crest, which is why it looks like a dent being dragged

   Off the patch it respawns at a random point on the rim circle of
   radius sea_disp_size, with a random heading and a fresh turn rate.

   IT RESPAWNS FAR MORE OFTEN THAN IT APPEARS TO. Measured over 4000
   ticks: one respawn every 13 ticks, and a mean radius of 747 of 1024.
   Because the respawn puts it exactly on the rim and then picks a
   heading at random, about half the time it steps straight back out
   again. Most of that churn is invisible: the rim circle passes through
   the +/-x vertices, so a diagonal spawn such as (724, 724) is outside
   the hexagon and its pushes land on cells no vertex reads. What shows
   is the occasional one that wanders inward and tracks across.

   Suppressed entirely when game_stat is 6, GAME_STAT_NAME, so the
   water holds still while a name is being entered.

   F5 toggles it here. The mechanism is the same as X with the mouse -
   repeated sea_push along a path - with the sign of the depth flipped.

10.4.  The Seed, And Why The Triangles Showed

   Without init_wave_high the mesh is visible as a diamond pattern of
   facets, worst on gentle wide waves, and no amount of texture
   filtering helps. The cause is not the filter and not the mesh.

   vertex_sea builds its normal from heights that have already been
   shifted >> 7, so the gradient is an integer and the smallest step it
   can take is one. On water that is locally flat the gradient is
   exactly zero across whole patches, every vertex in the patch gets the
   same texture coordinate, and a triangle whose three corners share a
   coordinate samples ONE texel over its whole area. Bilinear filtering
   cannot rescue that: it smooths the lookup, not a coordinate field
   that is already constant. Measured on a gentle wave:

   +-----------------------------+-------------+------------------+
   | state                       | distinct UV | flat-normal      |
   +=============================+=============+==================+
   | unseeded                    |          89 | 490 of 817       |
   +-----------------------------+-------------+------------------+
   | init_wave_high(20)          |         243 | 15 of 817        |
   +-----------------------------+-------------+------------------+
   | seeded, 120 ticks later     |          31 | 157 of 817       |
   +-----------------------------+-------------+------------------+
   | seeded, 400 ticks later     |           7 | 563 of 817       |
   +-----------------------------+-------------+------------------+

   So the game is never in the degenerate regime: init_sea seeds the
   whole buffer every time a screen is entered, and the chop keeps the
   gradient clear of the quantisation floor everywhere. The seed matters
   for the NORMALS, not for the motion, which is why it was easy to
   leave out and hard to spot.

   The field is biased positive and smaller than the nominal +/- high,
   about 5 to 18 world units for high = 20, because only one of the six
   accumulator slots is ever refreshed. See the note in sea.c.

   IT DECAYS. The damping eats the chop over a few hundred ticks and the
   facets come back, as the table shows. The game re-seeds on every
   screen entry; here M does it on demand, and Space clears it along
   with everything else.

   The gradient precision itself is still the original's. Computing the
   slope from unshifted heights would remove the floor outright and keep
   gentle water smooth indefinitely, but that is a divergence from the
   ROM and is not done.

11.  Open Questions

   ANSWERED, see 10.3 and 10.4: the sea_mode 1 screens are driven by
   move_sea's walker, one invisible point pushing a trough per tick, on
   top of the init_wave_high seed. Both are ported. ot_sea_report
   section 7 calls the injection "ambient push_sea ripples at a fixed
   centre", which is wrong on both counts - it wanders, and it is a
   single point. That report wants correcting.

   WHAT IS sea_offset ON THAT SCREEN? It is the visible water level and
   it is added to every baked Y. A wrong value puts the grid above or
   below the camera. Read it live rather than guessing a constant.
